Data at the scale of the single plant

The agronomic decisions that matter are taken one plant at a time. The rover works at that scale.

The fruiting zone, seen from the side

Fruit and symptoms sit on the vertical canopy wall, beneath the upper leaves. The rover captures them up close and at a constant distance as it travels the inter-row, in the position where they appear.

Surveys you can compare

Its own lighting makes acquisitions comparable in low-light hours, at dawn, at dusk and at night. Two passes weeks apart remain readable side by side.

The microclimate of your rows

Temperature, humidity, rainfall and leaf wetness recorded along the whole route. A profile of the vineyard rather than the single reading of a fixed station, which is what forecasting models ask for as input.

Every vine has a history

Every acquisition comes out dated and georeferenced, so the next visit returns to the same plant. It is the register of the vineyard, built season after season.

From the satellite to the single vine

The rover is the last step, not the first. The vineyard is read from above and the weather data are read first; only then do we come down to the plant. The three levels end up in a single periodic bulletin that says what is worth doing now.

  1. The whole vineyard, from the satellite

    A public satellite passes over the vineyard every few days and measures how much green there is. The record goes back to 2017: each season is compared with the previous ones and with the surrounding countryside, which shows whether a decline concerns your vineyard alone or the whole area, and where it is worth going to look.

  2. The risk, from the weather

    Temperature, humidity, rainfall and leaf wetness feed the forecasting models, which indicate when conditions favour downy and powdery mildew. This is the data that allows you to move before the disease becomes visible.

  3. The single plant, from the ground

    Close-range capture, on foot today and with the rover from spring 2027, reaches the individual vine. This is the scale at which leaf symptoms appear, such as the tiger-stripe pattern of esca, and the level that makes it possible to return to the same vine on the next visit.

A vineyard in the Marche

The satellite record reconstructed back to 2017, and a periodic bulletin that carries on season after season. In the latest issue, a vine with tiger-striped leaves photographed in the row was read together with the trend in canopy greenness and a week of heat. One single instruction came out of it: mark the diseased vines before leaf fall.

A vineyard in Emilia-Romagna

At the first visit the satellite had flagged nothing across the whole season, while a 350-metre walk along a single inter-row found six anomalies, three of them consistent with esca. This is not a failure of the satellite: one of its cells is ten metres by ten and holds around twenty vines, and one diseased plant among twenty healthy ones does not move the average. Confirmation on the wood is the agronomist's, but this is exactly the gap that ground-level surveying fills.

In development, patent application filed

UV-C light against powdery mildew, with no residue

Powdery mildew calls for a long series of treatments every season, organic vineyards included. We are developing a UV-C module to be mounted on the rover which, at night, exposes leaves and fruit to brief doses of ultraviolet light as it travels the row.

Vineyard trials carried out by international research show that, applied in darkness, this light suppresses powdery mildew without affecting vine growth or yield, and without leaving anything on the fruit. It is a concrete route to using fewer plant protection products, with a treatment carried out in the same hours of darkness in which the rover is designed to work.
